Why making things too easy destroys their perceived value: The Alchemy of Meaningful Friction in design and AI.

We keep optimizing the wrong end of the equation.

Whenever an engineer or spreadsheet optimizer looks at a human problem, their instinctive move is to eliminate every ounce of friction. Make it faster. Make it one-click. Make it zero-effort.

And yet, when you look closely at what humans actually treasure—from pour-over coffee to vinyl records, from hard-won craft to sacred ceremonies—value is almost never found in frictionless speed. Value is born in meaningful friction.

1. The Trap of Logical Efficiency

As Rory Sutherland often points out, a problem that seems logical on paper is rarely solved with logical answers. If logic worked on human beings, bicycles would be banned in cities and restaurants would only serve nutrient paste.

When you remove all friction from an experience:

  • You remove personal investment: What costs no effort commands zero emotional equity.
  • You destroy the psychological signal: In biology and culture, signaling theory proves that unforgeable cost (effort, time, craft) is the only proof of commitment.
  • You commoditize the outcome: If an AI or button can generate it in 200 milliseconds without your sweat, nobody can fall in love with it.

“A flower is simply a weed with an advertising budget; and a luxury product is merely an everyday utility with deliberate, high-status friction.”

2. The Behavioral Alchemy (The Reframe)

Don’t add more speed. Introduce curated, deliberate friction.

Look at the cake mix paradox from the 1950s. When instant cake mixes first launched requiring only water, sales flopped. Housewives felt like frauds. When the manufacturers changed the recipe so you had to crack a fresh egg into the bowl, sales skyrocketed. The egg added trivial physical work, but enormous psychological ownership.

In modern software, business, and daily creative life, we need more eggs to crack.

3. Real-World Mechanisms & Citations

  1. The Ikea Effect (Norton, Mochon, Ariely, 2012): Consumers place disproportionately high value on products they partially created or assembled themselves.
  2. Costly Signaling Theory (Zahavi, 1975 / Grafen, 1990): Signals that are cheap to produce carry near-zero informational reliability in social and economic markets.
  3. Choice Architecture & Satisficing (Simon, 1956; Kahneman, 2011): System 1 defaults to path of least resistance, but System 2 requires friction to ignite deep cognitive engagement and memory encoding.

4. A Door That Opens

Here is the operational move:

  • Stop asking: “How do we make this 10x faster?”
  • Start asking: “Where can we add the one point of meaningful friction that gives the user genuine pride of authorship?”

When you build products, write essays, or architect systems with Cursor and AI, do not let the AI do 100% of the thinking while you passively spectate. Inject your own raw conviction, voice, and critique into the loop.

The machine provides leverage; your human friction provides the soul.
