When did childhood become an entry-level corporate internship?

Look at a suburban family calendar on a Saturday. Color blocks for travel soccer. A violin slot that cannot slip. Coding club because someone at dinner said “AI will eat the unprepared.” The sandbox is still in the yard. Nobody has time to use it.

We tell ourselves this is love with a stopwatch. We tell ourselves that in a hyper-competitive world, every unoptimized hour is a theft from their future.

And yet the pattern underneath is colder: **by turning childhood into a resume-building sprint, parents often manufacture the fragility they are trying to prevent.**

## Goodhart’s law in the living room

In 1975, economist Charles Goodhart warned that statistical regularities collapse once you lean on them for control. The popular restatement is sharper: *when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.*

Apply that to the dinner table.

When grades, trophies, and elite-admission odds become the explicit target of parenting, they stop measuring what you actually wanted—curiosity, grit, judgment, character. The child learns the map of parental approval. They stop exploring the territory.

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan) is blunt about the mechanism: humans need autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Heap on surveillance and contingent praise, and intrinsic motivation thins out. The piano is no longer music. It is a performance review with keys.

Affluent, high-pressure contexts make this worse, not better. Luthar and colleagues documented “privileged but pressured” youth with elevated anxiety, depression, and substance risk—exactly the cohort whose parents optimized hardest.

## Carpenter parents, gardener children

Developmental psychologist Alison Gopnik draws the useful fork:

- **Carpenter parents** treat the child like lumber. Chisel toward a finished product. If the wood does not become the chair you sketched (doctor, founder, concert pianist), the carpenter failed.
- **Gardener parents** know they do not control the shape of the plant. They enrich soil, protect the perimeter, and allow a resilient ecosystem to root.

Carpentry feels responsible. It photographs well. Gardening looks like under-parenting to the neighbors. The irony is that creativity, leadership, and antifragile competence are non-linear. The kids who win the compliance race at nine are not automatically the adults who can invent under fog.

## The play deficit

Peter Gray’s work on the decline of free play is the quiet indictment of the optimized childhood. Across decades in developed countries, unsupervised play with other children fell hard. Across the same window, measures of anxiety, depression, and helplessness among young people rose.

Play is not a snack between achievements. It is where children negotiate rules, take calibrated risks, recover from small failures, and discover interests nobody assigned them. When adults referee every minute, the child never builds an internal compass. They become excellent at waiting for the next rubric.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has been explicit: play builds executive function and buffering relationships; pediatricians should defend unstructured play against the pressure to didacticize early childhood. That is not soft parenting. It is developmental engineering with better priors.

## One month off the treadmill

You do not need a philosophy seminar. You need calendar edits.

1. **Protect empty blocks.** Schedule unscheduled time. Boredom is fertilizer, not a defect.
2. **Praise autonomy and effort, not rank.** Ask what they tried, not where they placed.
3. **Allow micro-failures.** Forgotten homework, lost matches, crooked inventions—without the helicopter extraction.
4. **Cut one curated activity this month.** Not forever. Enough to feel the oxygen return.
5. **Be a gardener in public.** When someone asks what your child is “working toward,” answer with who they are becoming when nobody is scoring them.

The race will still exist. Markets are markets. The question is whether you turn your child’s nervous system into the racetrack surface.

Protect some empty time. Let the plant surprise you.
